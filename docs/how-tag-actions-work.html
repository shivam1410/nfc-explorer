NFC Explorer · how a tap becomes an action

The tag knows nothing

A question worth settling before anything else gets built on top of it: when you tap a tag, is the tag telling the phone what to do — or is the phone already the one who knows?

The short answer

We carry the data. The tag carries a serial number and nothing else.

Nothing is ever written to the tag. It is a name, not a note.

Every NFC tag ever made has a serial number burned into it at the factory. It cannot be changed, and the tag announces it to any phone that comes near — before any password, any handshake, any permission. That number is the only thing this app takes from a tag. Everything else — the label, what should happen, which timer to start, which message to open — is kept in the app, on your phone, filed under that number.

An everyday version of the same idea

A barcode doesn’t know the price

The barcode printed on a packet of biscuits contains no price. It is a number. The price lives in the shop’s computer, filed under that number. This is why a shop can change a price in one afternoon without reprinting a single packet — and why the same biscuits cost something different in a different shop, scanning the very same barcode.

What the app keeps

One line per tag

The whole of the app’s memory about your tags is a list like this one. A serial number, a name you chose so you can recognise it, and one thing to do. A tag can only ever have one entry — the serial number is the key, so saving a new action replaces the old one rather than piling up beside it.

Serial number (from the tag) Your name for it What a tap does
04 1C 4E 52 CE 7C 80 Desk card Starts a work timer, or stops the one already running
04 8A 22 90 3B 11 04 Bedside card Starts sleep tracking at night, ends it in the morning — same card, both ways
04 F2 61 3D 9D 04 A2 Fridge magnet Opens a WhatsApp chat with a message ready to send
04 B7 09 CC 77 1E 6B Bookmark Opens the book’s notes page

Note the second row. One card that starts and stops depends on knowing which of the two you meant — and that is a thing the app works out on the phone at the moment of the tap. A tag could never hold that, no matter what you wrote on it.

What happens when you tap — in order

Five steps, about a second

  1. The phone wakes the tag up. The tag has no battery. The phone’s radio powers it for as long as they are touching.
  2. The tag says its serial number. That is the entire conversation with the tag. Nothing is read from it, nothing written to it.
  3. The app checks that this is a real tap. Three things must all be true: the phone’s own system reported a tag, the tag is genuinely there and answering, and that serial number is one we know. If any one fails, nothing happens at all — no screen, no sound.
  4. The app looks the number up in its list. It finds one line: a name, and one thing to do.
  5. The app does it. Opens an app, opens a link, presses a button, or — the case worth understanding next — calls a service over the internet.

Where the password lives

The tag is a public thing

Step five is the one you asked about. When a tap starts a work timer, the app makes a call over the internet to the timer service — and that call needs a password. So the natural question is where that password sits.

Not on the tag. A tag has no lock on reading: anyone who brushes a phone against your bookmark can read every byte on it, and you would never know. So the password lives in the phone’s own encrypted store — the same kind of vault your banking app uses. It is not in the list above. It is not in the action. It never goes near the card.

Why it was built this way

Three reasons, in order of how much they mattered

It works on cards you cannot write to

A hotel key card, an old transit card, a card whose memory has been permanently locked — none of them will accept anything you try to write. All of them announce a serial number. So all of them work here, and a card you already own becomes usable without buying anything.

Nothing private sits on something anyone can read

A card offers no way to require a password before being read. Anything written there is public to every phone that passes it. Keeping the meaning on the phone means a lost bookmark reveals a number, and a number reveals nothing.

Behaviour changes without the tag present

Renaming a tag, pointing it at a different book, changing what it does — all of it happens in the app, and none of it needs the card in your hand. The same edit then syncs to your other phone.

One thing to be clear about

This app can write to tags. There is a whole screen for it — write text, write raw bytes, wipe a card — because the app was originally built to inspect cards and see what was on them. That is a separate tool for a separate job.

It is not how the tap actions work, and turning it off tomorrow would not break a single one of them. Worth saying plainly, because “the app writes to tags” and “the tags carry the data” sound like the same sentence, and only the first one is true.
